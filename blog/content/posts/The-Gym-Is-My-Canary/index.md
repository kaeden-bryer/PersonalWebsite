![A canary in a coal mine](/canary.jpg)
I realized a while ago that the gym is my canary. In the 1900's, canaries were used to detect carbon monoxide in mines. With a much lower body mass, the invisible carbon monoxide would kill the birds before humans, and so when canaries died, miners could assume they were soon to follow. In a similar vein, whenever going to the gym died as a habit, the rest of my habits and indeed my entire system were soon to follow.

But canaries were intended as sacrifices to begin with, whereas working out is a genuine habit I'm trying to uphold. Yes, whenever I lose the gym, I can assume that other habits will follow. However, it's less so causation and more so a chain of events:

1. I become motivated to start new habits. Going to the gym is always one of them.
2. I become engrossed in things besides the gym. These become priority.
3. Eventually, I lose the gym. However, I'm still moving full speed ahead
4. I gas out. I begin to drop other habits
5. Burn out.
6. Repeat

I don't think it's accurate to say quitting the gym is a sign that I'm losing my motivation. On the contrary, beginning the gym is a sign that I'm regaining my motivation. The gym is not my canary; it's my rocket booster.

> [!Idea] The Gym is Not My Canary; It's my Rocket Booster
> Every time I start going to the gym, it's signaling a resurgence of motivation, not the death of it

![Rocket boosters](/booster.jpg)

It's not entirely incorrect to say the gym is my canary, but the gym doesn't die because I lose motivation––it dies because I become unhealthily obsessed with productivity. My productivity begins with the gym, but I always end up piling on too much more. Eventually, I burn out, until I finally regain the motivation to start again. Toxic productivity is my carbon monoxide.

When I'm unable to make time for the gym, I should be asking, "Am I overloading myself?" The answer is probably yes. I should then reflect on where my time is being spent, and whether there's anything I should be saying no to that I'm not.
